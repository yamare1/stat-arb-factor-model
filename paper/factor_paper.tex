\title{\textbf{Cross-Sectional Ranking Power Without Alpha:}\\
\large A Walk-Forward Study of Five Price-Based Equity Factors, 2006--2023}

\author{Yonathan Amare}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
I test whether five standard price-based factors --- long-horizon momentum,
short-term reversal, realized volatility, volatility-scaled momentum, and
medium-horizon momentum --- generate market-neutral alpha in the S\&P 500
cross-section over 2006--2023. Using a 2.0-million-observation panel, rolling
walk-forward validation with purged boundaries, and a beta-neutral long-short
portfolio net of transaction costs, I find they do not.

The result is more specific than a simple failure. First, only realized
volatility carries standalone predictive power (IC $0.043$, $t \approx 2.84$);
the three momentum variants are statistically indistinguishable from zero.
Second, two of the five factors correlate at $0.91$, and the fitted linear model
assigns them large opposing coefficients that cancel --- deleting three
redundant factors nearly doubles out-of-sample information coefficient, from
$0.0236$ to $0.0448$, and raises the share of positive folds from 71\% to 86\%.
Third, and most importantly, the improved signal still loses money. The top
predicted decile outperforms the bottom by $1.40\%$ per month, but every decile
has a positive mean return, and the spread is fully explained by a long-leg beta
of $\approx 1.3$ against a short-leg beta of $\approx 0.6$. Imposing beta
neutrality forces approximately $30/70$ leg weights, which is a structural short
of the equity risk premium; the resulting portfolio returns $-1.46\%$ annually
with a Sharpe ratio of $-0.20$.

The paper's contribution is methodological rather than strategic: it documents
a case where cross-sectional ranking power is real, statistically significant,
and economically worthless, and it identifies the specific mechanism.
A by-product of the design quantifies survivorship bias in the commonly used
``current index constituents'' universe at roughly 9 percentage points per year.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Factor investing rests on the claim that observable characteristics predict
relative returns. Since \citet{fama1993} the literature has catalogued dozens of
such characteristics, and \citet{mclean2016} document that published anomalies
decay substantially after publication --- consistent with arbitrage capital
competing the return away.

This paper asks a narrow version of the question using only publicly available
data and five widely documented signals. The finding is negative, and the value
lies in the decomposition: the signal does rank the cross-section, the ranking
is statistically significant, and it nevertheless produces no investable return.
Understanding why requires separating three things that are easy to conflate ---
predictive correlation, portfolio construction, and factor exposure.

Three design decisions are worth stating up front, since each changes the answer
materially:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Information coefficient is computed within each date and then
averaged}, never pooled. Pooling across a multi-year panel mixes cross-sectional
with time-series variation and overstates what a cross-sectional model achieves.
\item \textbf{Training and test windows are purged} by the target horizon.
Without a 21-day embargo, terminal training observations have targets extending
into the test period \citep{lopezdeprado2018}.
\item \textbf{The portfolio is beta-neutral, not dollar-neutral.} As Section~6
shows, this single choice reverses the sign of the strategy's return.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data}

\subsection{Universe}

Constituents are the current S\&P 500 membership list (503 tickers), of which
seven fail to download because they listed after the sample window. Daily
split- and dividend-adjusted closing prices are retrieved from Yahoo Finance for
2005-01-01 through 2024-01-01, yielding 4{,}781 trading days.

The completeness filter deserves comment. A naive threshold --- requiring, say,
95\% of available days --- would exclude every firm that listed after 2006 and
shrink the universe to long-tenured incumbents, worsening the survivorship
problem the filter is supposed to be neutral about. Instead I require a
three-year minimum history and allow names to enter the panel as they become
available. The universe grows from 392 names in 2005 to 488 from 2020 onward,
with no discontinuities.

\subsection{Survivorship bias}

The sample is severely survivor-biased and the bias is not correctable with free
data. Membership is the 2026 index applied retrospectively, so the 2005
cross-section consists of 392 firms that were public in 2005 \emph{and} remained
in the index two decades later. Delisted, acquired, and demoted firms are absent.

The magnitude is measurable. The universe-average 21-day return is $1.41\%$,
annualizing to $18.36\%$, against $9.58\%$ for SPY over the identical window ---
a gap of $\mathbf{8.78}$ \textbf{percentage points per year}. This overstates
pure survivorship bias, since the universe is equal-weighted while SPY is
capitalization-weighted, and the small-cap tilt of equal weighting contributes
part of the difference. It is therefore an upper bound. All return levels in
this paper are inflated by some fraction of it; long-short spreads are less
affected, since the bias applies to both legs.

\section{Factor construction}

Five factors are computed from daily returns and cross-sectionally
rank-normalized within each date to $\mathrm{pct\_rank} - 0.5$:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Factor definitions}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Factor} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Window} & \textbf{Reference} \\
\midrule
\texttt{mom\_12\_1}    & $\sum_{t-252}^{t-21} r$      & 12m less 1m & \citet{jegadeesh1993} \\
\texttt{reversal}      & $-\sum_{t-21}^{t} r$         & 1m          & \citet{jegadeesh1990} \\
\texttt{volatility}    & $\sigma_{21}(r)$             & 1m          & \citet{ang2006} \\
\texttt{vol\_adj\_mom} & \texttt{mom\_12\_1}$/\sigma_{21}$ & 12m     & risk-scaled trend \\
\texttt{mom\_3}        & $\sum_{t-63}^{t} r$          & 3m          & medium-horizon trend \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Rank normalization is a substantive choice, not cosmetic. The models are asked
to order stocks against one another on a given date, so market-wide shifts in
raw factor levels are noise. Ranking also renders the features scale-free,
eliminating the need for a separate standardizer whose parameters would
otherwise be fitted across the full sample --- itself a subtle leak.

The prediction target is the 21-day forward return, constructed as
\texttt{rolling(21).sum().shift(-21)}.

The resulting panel contains \textbf{2{,}011{,}701 stock-day observations}
spanning 2006-01-03 to 2023-11-29, with a median of 454 names per date. The
252-day momentum window consumes 2005; the forward target truncates the final
21 days.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Walk-forward validation}

A single chronological split evaluates one regime. I instead train on 756
trading days ($\approx$3 years), skip 21 days, and test on the following 252
days, advancing one year per iteration. This produces \textbf{14 folds with
non-overlapping test windows}, February 2009 through February 2023.

The 21-day skip implements purging in the sense of \citet{lopezdeprado2018}.
Because the target spans 21 days, the final training observations would
otherwise carry information from the test period.

A limitation: the first training block consumes 2006--2008, so the earliest test
window opens after the financial crisis. The models are evaluated on the
recovery, never on the crisis itself.

\subsection{Models}

Ridge regression ($\alpha = 1.0$) serves as the linear specification. Gradient
boosting (XGBoost; 300 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.05, subsample 0.8,
column-subsample 0.8) provides the nonlinear comparison. Hyperparameters were
fixed a priori rather than tuned, to avoid consuming the out-of-sample data
through selection.

\subsection{Evaluation}

The information coefficient is the Spearman rank correlation between predicted
and realized forward returns, computed \emph{within each date} and then averaged:
\[
\overline{\mathrm{IC}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\rho_{\text{Spearman}}\!\left(\hat{y}_{t}, y_{t}\right).
\]
For a return series, the $t$-statistic on annualized alpha is approximately
$\mathrm{SR}\sqrt{\text{years}}$. At a Sharpe ratio of $0.3$, roughly 44 years
are required to reach $t=2$ --- a constraint that bounds what any study of this
length can establish and which motivates the 18-year sample.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Walk-forward performance}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Walk-forward IC, five factors, 14 folds}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Mean IC} & \textbf{Std} & \textbf{Folds $>0$} & \textbf{$t$} \\
\midrule
Ridge   & $+0.0236$ & $0.0589$ & 71\% & $1.50$ \\
XGBoost & $+0.0121$ & $0.0212$ & 79\% & $2.14$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Ridge posts the higher mean but is markedly less stable, and its average is
dominated by one fold: 2009 returns $+0.176$, seven times the overall mean.
Excluding it, Ridge's average falls to approximately $+0.011$. The 2016 fold is
the mirror image at $-0.085$. Both are the same mechanism --- the factor set
loads on high-volatility names, which rebound sharply in a recovery and decay in
a calm market.

XGBoost exhibits neither extreme. Its fold-level information ratio is $0.57$
against Ridge's $0.40$, and it clears $t=2$ where Ridge does not.

Ridge's higher mean is not evidence of a better model. A single fold contributes
more than half of its average, and its fold-level $t$-statistic ($1.50$) fails
to clear conventional significance where XGBoost's ($2.14$) does. Stability
across regimes, not mean IC, distinguishes the two.

\texttt{mom\_12\_1} and \texttt{vol\_adj\_mom} correlate at $0.91$: dividing by
volatility barely perturbs the cross-sectional ordering, so the two are
effectively one variable entered twice. \texttt{mom\_3} correlates $-0.53$ with
\texttt{reversal}, both being constructed from overlapping recent-return windows
of opposite sign. Five nominal factors reduce to approximately two independent
signals.

The fitted coefficients display the consequence directly. In the terminal fold
Ridge assigns $-0.0305$ to \texttt{mom\_12\_1} and $+0.0280$ to
\texttt{vol\_adj\_mom} --- two inputs correlated at $0.91$ receiving large
opposing weights that nearly cancel. The model is fitting the residual
difference between near-identical variables, which is close to pure noise. This
is the mechanical source of the fold-to-fold instability, and it explains the
2009 outlier more parsimoniously than any regime narrative.

\subsection{The effect of removing redundant factors}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Walk-forward IC: five factors versus two}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Specification} & \textbf{Mean IC} & \textbf{Std} & \textbf{Folds $>0$} & \textbf{$t$} \\
\midrule
5-factor Ridge & $+0.0236$ & $0.0589$ & 71\% & $\approx 1.50$ \\
2-factor Ridge (\texttt{volatility}, \texttt{reversal})
               & $\mathbf{+0.0448}$ & $\mathbf{0.0513}$ & $\mathbf{86\%}$ & $\approx 3.26$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Discarding three of five factors nearly doubles mean out-of-sample IC, reduces
its dispersion, and raises the positive-fold rate by fifteen percentage points.
The multicollinearity diagnosis is thus confirmed out-of-sample: the removed
factors were not contributing information, they were destabilizing the fit.

The $t$-statistic treats the fourteen fold means as independent. Test windows do
not overlap, but training windows do, so it should be read as directional.

\section{Portfolio results and the source of the spread}

\subsection{Construction}

At each month-end the model's predictions are ranked cross-sectionally. The top
quintile is held long and the bottom quintile short until the next rebalance.
Positions are lagged one day, so the portfolio trades on the prior day's signal.

Leg weights impose \emph{beta} neutrality rather than dollar neutrality. With
$\beta_L$ and $\beta_S$ the mean trailing 252-day betas of the two legs,
\[
w_L = \frac{\beta_S}{\beta_L + \beta_S}, \qquad
w_S = \frac{\beta_L}{\beta_L + \beta_S},
\]
which sets net beta to zero at unit gross exposure. Transaction costs of 16 bps
(5 spread, 10 impact, 1 commission) are applied to one-way turnover.

Beta neutralization is effective --- residual beta is under $0.01$ with
$R^2 \approx 0$. Both specifications lose money. The improvement in IC from
dropping redundant factors does not translate into return.

The drawdown profile is a twelve-year grind (peak 2010-04, trough 2022-04)
rather than a shock; 48.1\% of days are positive; excess kurtosis of $7.25$ with
the worst days clustered in March--April 2020 reflects gap risk in a long-short
book during a volatility spike. Inverting the signal produces a Sharpe ratio of
$0.321$ with $t = 1.20$, also insignificant --- so the strategy is noise centred
slightly below zero, not a reliably incorrect model that could be traded in
reverse.

\begin{figure}[H]

\label{fig:deciles}
\end{figure}

The spread from bottom to top decile is $+1.396\%$ per 21 days, approximately
monotonic outside a flat region in deciles 3--6. The signal ranks the
cross-section and the ranking holds in the tails, ruling out the hypothesis that
it predicts only the interior of the distribution.

\textbf{Every decile has a positive mean return.} That is what defeats the
strategy. Long-leg beta is approximately $1.3$ against a short-leg beta of
approximately $0.6$, so beta neutrality forces weights near $30/70$. Applying
those to the traded quintiles:
\[
\underbrace{0.30 \times 2.353\%}_{\text{long}} \; - \;
\underbrace{0.70 \times 1.308\%}_{\text{short}}
\;=\; 0.706\% - 0.916\% \;=\; -0.210\% \text{ per 21 days} \;\approx\; -2.5\%/\text{yr},
\]
which reproduces the realized $-1.46\%$ to within costs and time variation in
the betas.

\textbf{The decile spread is compensation for market beta, not alpha.} Imposing
beta neutrality converts a levered short position in low-beta stocks into a
structural short of the equity risk premium, and that carry is negative.

This is the betting-against-beta relationship of \citet{frazzini2014} operating
in reverse. BAB is long low-beta levered and short high-beta; a
volatility-ranking signal does the opposite, so its beta-neutral form is
negative by construction. Its dollar-neutral form earns a positive return, but
that return is market exposure rather than alpha.

\section{Conclusion}

Five widely documented price-based factors do not produce market-neutral alpha
in the large-capitalization US cross-section over 2006--2023. The specific
findings:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Only realized volatility carries standalone predictive power
(IC $0.043$, $t \approx 2.84$). The three momentum variants are indistinguishable
from zero.
\item Redundant factors are harmful, not merely useless. Two of five correlate
at $0.91$ and receive cancelling coefficients; removing three factors nearly
doubles out-of-sample IC.
\item Mean IC alone is a poor model-selection criterion. Ridge posts the higher average but fails significance and depends on one fold; XGBoost is lower but stable and significant.
\item Cross-sectional ranking power does not imply alpha. A $1.40\%$ monthly
decile spread is fully accounted for by a beta differential, and vanishes under
beta neutrality.
\item Neither specification is investable, and neither is the inverted signal.
\end{enumerate}

The methodological point generalizes beyond these five factors. A researcher
evaluating only pooled IC, on a short sample, with a dollar-neutral portfolio,
would have concluded that this factor set works. Each of those three choices is
individually defensible and collectively produces a false positive.
